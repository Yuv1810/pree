# Review of Literature

---

## 2.1 Overview

This chapter presents a comprehensive and critical review of the empirical and theoretical literature pertaining to the three core constructs of the present study: **self-compassion**, **psychological distress**, and **perceived social support**. The review is organized thematically, progressing from individual construct-level evidence to dyadic relationships and, finally, to the interactive (moderation) models that form the theoretical backbone of this investigation.

---

## 2.2 Self-Compassion and Psychological Distress

### 2.2.1 Meta-Analytic and Systematic Evidence

The relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress has been extensively documented across meta-analytic, systematic, and individual empirical investigations. The evidence overwhelmingly supports a robust **negative association** between self-compassion and indicators of psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and stress.

**Ferrari et al. (2019)** conducted a landmark meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining the efficacy of self-compassion interventions on psychosocial outcomes. Synthesizing data from multiple RCTs, the authors reported significant effect sizes for the reduction of depression, anxiety, and psychological distress following self-compassion-based interventions. The meta-analysis provided Level I evidence (the highest level of evidence in the evidence hierarchy) that self-compassion interventions are causally effective in reducing psychological distress.

**Galino Buen et al. (2026)** extended this meta-analytic work with a focused examination of mindfulness- and self-compassion-based interventions (MBSCIs) among university students. This meta-analysis is particularly relevant to the present study as it specifically addresses the university student population. The authors reported significant reductions in psychological distress among students who participated in MBSCIs, with effect sizes ranging from small to moderate. Importantly, the analysis also identified predictors of change, including baseline distress severity and intervention duration, providing insights into the conditions under which self-compassion interventions are most effective.

**Wang et al. (2025)** conducted a systematic review examining the mechanisms underlying the relationship between self-compassion and psychological outcomes in adult populations. The review identified several key mediating pathways, including:
- Cognitive reappraisal and adaptive coping
- Reduced rumination and self-criticism
- Enhanced emotion regulation
- Increased psychological flexibility
- Reduced shame and guilt

These findings suggest that self-compassion does not operate through a single pathway but rather through a constellation of interconnected cognitive, affective, and behavioral mechanisms.

### 2.2.2 Individual Empirical Studies

At the individual study level, a rich body of evidence corroborates the meta-analytic findings:

**Liu and Wang (2026)** examined academic burnout and depression among college students, finding that self-compassion served as a significant mediator of this relationship. Students with higher levels of self-compassion reported lower levels of depression even when experiencing substantial academic burnout, suggesting that self-compassion provides a cognitive-emotional buffer against the internalization of academic stress.

**Liu et al. (2024)** investigated the relationships between self-compassion and psychological flourishing among college students, demonstrating that self-compassion, mediated by hope, contributes to psychological flourishing. Emotion regulation was identified as a moderating variable, indicating that the benefits of self-compassion for psychological flourishing are contingent upon the individual's capacity for emotion regulation.

**Li et al. (2022)** conducted a longitudinal study examining self-compassion as a buffer against the psychological distress associated with perceived discrimination among socioeconomically disadvantaged emerging adults. The study found that self-compassion significantly attenuated the adverse effects of perceived discrimination on psychological distress over time, providing temporal evidence for the protective function of self-compassion.

**Dehshiri (2024)** explored the relationships between self-compassion, anxiety, and depression symptoms, with shame and guilt examined as mediating variables. The study found that self-compassion was negatively associated with both anxiety and depression symptoms, and that this relationship was partially mediated by reductions in shame and guilt. This finding highlights the affective mechanisms through which self-compassion mitigates psychological distress.

**Liu and Hu (2023)** demonstrated that self-compassion both mediates and moderates the association between harsh parenting and depressive symptoms among Chinese adolescents, illustrating the dual role of self-compassion as both an explanatory mechanism and a boundary condition in the development of depressive symptoms.

### 2.2.3 Self-Compassion in the Indian Context

Research on self-compassion within the Indian cultural context is emerging but remains limited:

**Jain et al. (2025)** conducted a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effects of an online self-compassion intervention on Indian students. The results indicated significant improvements in self-compassion scores and corresponding reductions in psychological distress symptoms among the intervention group compared to the control group. This study is particularly significant as it provides the first RCT-level evidence for the efficacy of self-compassion interventions among Indian university students.

**Iyer et al. (2022)** examined the role of self-compassion in mediating the relationships between internet addiction, aggression, and psychological well-being among Indian populations. The findings indicated that self-compassion served as a significant mediator, with higher levels of self-compassion associated with reduced aggression and improved psychological well-being, even among individuals with problematic internet use.

**Hossain et al. (2025)** explored the role of psychological well-being and self-esteem among higher education students in West Bengal, India. Although self-compassion was not the primary variable, the study's findings regarding the determinants of psychological well-being in the Indian higher education context provide relevant contextual evidence for the present investigation.

## 2.3 Perceived Social Support and Psychological Distress

### 2.3.1 Theoretical and Empirical Foundations

The relationship between perceived social support and psychological distress has been one of the most extensively researched associations in the psychological literature. Theoretical models, including the **Stress-Buffering Model** (Cohen & Wills, 1985) and the **Main Effect Model**, provide complementary frameworks for understanding this relationship.

**Rathakrishnan et al. (2022)** investigated perceived social support, coping strategies, and psychological distress among university students in Sabah, Malaysia, during the COVID-19 pandemic. The study found that perceived social support was significantly negatively correlated with psychological distress and was associated with the use of adaptive coping strategies. Importantly, the study highlighted the role of social support in promoting social sustainability within educational institutions during crisis periods.

**Shelton et al. (2017)** examined perceived social support and mental health outcomes among college students, with cultural orientations examined as moderating variables. The findings demonstrated that the relationship between perceived social support and mental health varied as a function of cultural orientation, with collectivistic orientations amplifying the protective effects of social support. This finding is particularly relevant for the Indian context, which is characterized by collectivistic cultural values.

**Ee et al. (2026)** investigated perceived social support and subjective happiness among undergraduate counseling students in Malaysia, finding that perceived social support was a significant predictor of subjective happiness, with self-esteem serving as a mediating variable. The study underscored the importance of cultivating supportive social networks within educational environments.

### 2.3.2 Social Support and Psychological Distress in the Indian Context

**Basak and Sinha (2020)** examined the association between interpersonal social support and perceived depression among undergraduate college students in Kolkata during the COVID-19 lockdown. The study found that social support was negatively associated with depression, with students who reported higher levels of interpersonal support experiencing lower levels of depressive symptoms. This study provides direct evidence for the protective role of social support among Indian university students.

**Stanley and Mettilda Bhuvaneswari (2016)** explored stress, anxiety, resilience, and coping among social work students in India. While not exclusively focused on perceived social support, the study highlighted the role of social and institutional support systems in promoting resilience and adaptive coping among Indian students facing academic and fieldwork-related stressors.

**Das et al. (2023)** documented the magnitude of psychological distress among medical and non-medical students in West Bengal during the late phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, providing contextual evidence for the elevated distress levels that necessitate the identification of protective factors such as social support.

### 2.3.3 Social Support Across Diverse Populations

**Kurniawan et al. (2024)** examined the association between sources of social support (family, friends, significant others) and depression among nursing students during the COVID-19 pandemic. The study found that different sources of support had differential associations with depression, with family support exhibiting the strongest protective effect.

**Yıldırım et al. (2026)** demonstrated that perceived social support and psychological flexibility mediate the relationship between positive solitude and psychological well-being among Turkish adults, illustrating the complex interplay between social support and intrapersonal resources in determining psychological outcomes.

**Zhang et al. (2025)** employed latent profile analysis to examine the predictive roles of self-compassion, perceived social support, and psychological flexibility in early maladaptive schemas among college students. The study identified distinct profiles of students based on their levels of these protective factors, with profiles characterized by high self-compassion and social support exhibiting the most adaptive schema configurations.

## 2.4 Self-Compassion and Perceived Social Support

### 2.4.1 The Intrapersonal–Interpersonal Nexus

The relationship between self-compassion (an intrapersonal resource) and perceived social support (an interpersonal resource) has received growing research attention, with evidence suggesting a positive association and potential synergistic effects.

**Tannous-Haddad et al. (2024)** tested a moderated mediation model examining perceived social support and psychological well-being, with self-compassion as a mediator and culture as a moderator. The study found that self-compassion mediated the relationship between perceived social support and psychological well-being, and that this mediating effect varied across cultural groups. This finding is particularly relevant to the present study as it provides empirical precedent for examining the interplay between self-compassion and social support in determining psychological outcomes.

**Zhou et al. (2022)** examined how perceived social support promotes psychological well-being among nursing students, with self-compassion and professional self-concept serving as explanatory mediators. The study demonstrated that perceived social support enhances self-compassion, which in turn promotes psychological well-being, suggesting a cascading protective mechanism from interpersonal resources to intrapersonal resources to psychological outcomes.

**Barclay (2024)** investigated the buffering effect of self-compassion on the strained relation between masculinity and social support. The study found that self-compassion moderates the relationship between masculine norm adherence and willingness to seek social support, suggesting that self-compassion can facilitate the utilization of social support even among populations that typically underutilize such resources.

**Masoumi et al. (2022)** examined self-compassion as the factor explaining the relationship between perceived social support and emotional self-regulation in the psychological well-being of breast cancer survivors. The study found that self-compassion mediated the relationship between perceived social support and emotional self-regulation, providing evidence that social support facilitates self-compassion, which in turn enhances adaptive emotion regulation.

### 2.4.2 Combined Effects on Psychological Outcomes

**Ross and Ross (2025)** investigated distress, social support, and self-compassion in relation to mental health among college students. The study found that both social support and self-compassion were independently associated with better mental health outcomes, with self-compassion exhibiting a stronger unique contribution. The interaction between social support and self-compassion was suggestive of complementary protective mechanisms.

**Yang et al. (2023)** examined perceived social support and procrastination among college students, with self-compassion and negative emotions serving as sequential mediators. The study found that perceived social support reduces procrastination by enhancing self-compassion, which in turn reduces negative emotions. This sequential mediation model illustrates how interpersonal and intrapersonal resources operate in concert to influence behavioral outcomes.

**Yang et al. (2023b)** investigated perceived social support, depressive symptoms, self-compassion, and mobile phone addiction in a moderated mediation model. The findings indicated that self-compassion moderates the mediating effect of depressive symptoms in the relationship between perceived social support and mobile phone addiction, providing empirical evidence for the moderating role of self-compassion in social support pathways.

## 2.5 The Moderation Model: Self-Compassion × Perceived Social Support → Psychological Distress

### 2.5.1 Theoretical Rationale for Moderation

The proposed moderation model is grounded in the theoretical proposition that the protective effect of self-compassion on psychological distress is not uniform but varies as a function of the individual's perceived social support context. This proposition draws upon:

- **Conservation of Resources Theory** (Hobfoll, 1989): Resources do not operate in isolation; they form "resource caravans" in which the possession of one resource facilitates the acquisition and effective utilization of other resources. Self-compassion (intrapersonal) and social support (interpersonal) may form a resource caravan in which the presence of social support amplifies the protective efficacy of self-compassion.

- **Ecological Systems Theory** (Bronfenbrenner, 1979): Individual-level protective factors (self-compassion) operate within nested social systems (microsystem: family, friends; mesosystem: peer networks; exosystem: institutional support). The efficacy of individual-level factors is shaped by the social-contextual conditions in which they are embedded.

### 2.5.2 Empirical Evidence for Moderation

**Hayes et al. (2023)** examined athletic identity and psychological distress among student-athletes, with social support and self-compassion investigated as moderating variables. The study found that both social support and self-compassion moderated the relationship between athletic identity and psychological distress, providing direct evidence for the moderating role of these variables in the context of identity-related stressors.

**Habibi et al. (2023)** examined the mediating role of self-compassion in the relationship between perceived social support and distress tolerance among parents of children with phenylketonuria. While the study employed a mediation framework, the findings are relevant to the moderation hypothesis as they demonstrate the interconnected roles of self-compassion and social support in determining distress-related outcomes.

**Nas (2025)** examined the moderating effect of self-compassion on compassion fatigue and satisfaction among counselors, demonstrating that self-compassion moderates the impact of compassion fatigue on professional satisfaction. Although the population differs from university students, the study provides methodological and theoretical precedent for examining self-compassion as a variable in moderation models.

**Hodes et al. (2022)** found that self-compassion mediates the association between trait anxiety and smartphone attachment, with generational differences (digital natives vs. digital immigrants) serving as a moderating variable. This study illustrates the importance of examining boundary conditions that modify the relationships between self-compassion and psychological outcomes.

### 2.5.3 Interventions and Applied Evidence

**Stutts (2022)** reviewed the literature on increasing self-compassion and provided recommendations for intervention design. The review highlighted that effective self-compassion interventions incorporate elements of social connection and support, suggesting that social support contexts may enhance the effectiveness of self-compassion cultivation.

**Brat et al. (2017)** described self-compassion as a wellness intervention for millennial college students, arguing that self-compassion-based interventions should be integrated into college counseling services. The authors emphasized the importance of creating supportive institutional environments that facilitate the development of self-compassion.

**Cădariu et al. (2025)** examined self-compassion and student well-being with attention to psychological mechanisms and counseling implications. The study highlighted the role of self-compassion in promoting student well-being through adaptive coping and emotion regulation, and recommended that counseling interventions address both self-compassion and social support to maximize therapeutic outcomes.

**Kroshus et al. (2021)** examined stress, self-compassion, and well-being during the transition to college, finding that self-compassion was associated with better adjustment and well-being during this critical developmental transition. The study underscored the importance of fostering self-compassion during the early college period when social support networks are being (re)established.

**Rakhshani et al. (2025)** examined the effect of mindfulness-based stress reduction on self-compassion and parent-child relationship quality, demonstrating that mindfulness-based interventions enhance both self-compassion and relational quality, providing evidence for the bidirectional relationship between intrapersonal and interpersonal resources.

**Clarke (2024)** investigated emerging adult life satisfaction and mental health, with self-compassion and social support examined as mediating variables. The study found that both self-compassion and social support significantly mediated the relationship between life satisfaction and mental health among emerging adults, providing evidence that these variables operate as complementary protective mechanisms.

## 2.6 Summary of Literature Gaps

Based on the comprehensive review presented above, the following critical gaps in the literature are identified:

| # | Gap | Evidence Base |
|---|-----|---------------|
| 1 | **Limited moderation studies:** Few studies have explicitly tested perceived social support as a moderator of the self-compassion–distress relationship. Most studies employ mediation frameworks. | Ferrari et al. (2019); Ross & Ross (2025) |
| 2 | **Insufficient Indian evidence:** The majority of research has been conducted in Western and East Asian contexts. Indian university students remain critically underrepresented. | Jain et al. (2025); Iyer et al. (2022); Hossain et al. (2025) |
| 3 | **Post-pandemic lacuna:** Limited research examines the interaction of self-compassion and social support in the post-pandemic psychosocial landscape. | Das et al. (2023); Tang et al. (2020) |
| 4 | **Intervention design gap:** Without understanding moderation effects, intervention design cannot be optimally tailored to individual social support contexts. | Stutts (2022); Brat et al. (2017) |
| 5 | **Multidimensional social support:** Most studies treat social support as a unitary construct; the differential moderating effects of family, friend, and significant other support remain unexplored. | Kurniawan et al. (2024); Ee et al. (2026) |

---

## 2.7 Conclusion

The extant literature provides strong evidence for the independent and interactive roles of self-compassion and perceived social support in predicting psychological distress among university students. However, the specific moderation hypothesis—that perceived social support moderates the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress—remains largely untested, particularly within the Indian university context. The present study addresses this gap by proposing a rigorous, theory-driven investigation of the moderation model, with implications for both theoretical advancement and applied intervention design.
