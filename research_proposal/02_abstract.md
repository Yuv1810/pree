## Self-Compassion and Psychological Distress among University Students: The Moderating Role of Perceived Social Support

---

University students globally are experiencing unprecedented levels of psychological distress, exacerbated by academic pressures, socioeconomic transitions, and the lingering psychosocial aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic (Tang et al., 2020; Das et al., 2023). Psychological distress—characterized by elevated symptoms of anxiety, depression, and emotional dysregulation—has emerged as a critical concern in higher education, with implications for academic performance, social functioning, and long-term mental health trajectories (Stanley & Mettilda Bhuvaneswari, 2016; Hossain et al., 2025). While considerable research has examined individual-level protective factors, a nuanced understanding of how these factors interact to buffer against psychological distress remains inadequate, particularly in the Indian university context.

**Self-compassion**, defined as the capacity to extend warmth, understanding, and non-judgmental awareness toward oneself during instances of suffering or perceived inadequacy (Neff, 2003), has garnered substantial empirical attention as a robust predictor of psychological well-being and a protective factor against psychological distress (Ferrari et al., 2019; Galino Buen et al., 2026). Meta-analytic evidence indicates that self-compassion-based interventions significantly reduce psychological distress among university students (Galino Buen et al., 2026), and self-compassion has been shown to mediate the relationship between adverse psychosocial experiences and negative mental health outcomes (Liu & Wang, 2026; Wang et al., 2025). However, the mechanisms through which self-compassion operates, particularly in interaction with social-contextual variables, remain underexplored.

**Perceived social support**—the subjective appraisal of the availability and adequacy of support from significant others, peers, and family (Zimet et al., 1988)—has been consistently identified as a key determinant of psychological well-being among university students (Rathakrishnan et al., 2022; Tannous-Haddad et al., 2024). Research suggests that perceived social support may not only exert a direct protective effect against distress but may also moderate the efficacy of intrapersonal resources such as self-compassion (Barclay, 2024; Ross & Ross, 2025). That is, the protective benefits of self-compassion on psychological distress may vary as a function of students' perceived levels of social support—a hypothesis that warrants rigorous empirical examination.

The present study proposes a **cross-sectional, quantitative research design** to investigate the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress among university students in India, with perceived social support examined as a moderating variable. A stratified random sample of **400 university students** (aged 18–30 years) will be recruited from multiple universities across West Bengal and neighboring regions of India. Data will be collected using standardized, validated psychometric instruments: the **Self-Compassion Scale (SCS; Neff, 2003)**, the **Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10; Kessler et al., 2002)**, and the **Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; Zimet et al., 1988)**. Hierarchical multiple regression with moderation analysis (using Hayes' PROCESS macro, Model 1) will constitute the primary analytic strategy, supplemented by Pearson's correlation, independent-samples t-tests, and one-way ANOVA to examine demographic variations.

It is hypothesized that (a) self-compassion will be negatively associated with psychological distress, (b) perceived social support will be negatively associated with psychological distress, (c) self-compassion will positively correlate with perceived social support, and (d) perceived social support will significantly moderate the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress, such that the inverse relationship between self-compassion and distress will be stronger among students with higher perceived social support. The findings are expected to contribute to the theoretical understanding of intrapersonal–interpersonal protective factor interactions, inform the design of targeted psychosocial interventions, and provide evidence-based recommendations for university counseling services, particularly in the Indian higher education context where empirical data remain sparse (Jain et al., 2025; Iyer et al., 2022).
