## 1.1 Background of the Study

The mental health of university students has emerged as a paramount public health concern worldwide. The transition to higher education represents a critical developmental period marked by significant academic, social, and psychological challenges (Kroshus et al., 2021). Students navigate demanding coursework, financial pressures, identity formation, relationship complexities, and, increasingly, the pervasive influence of digital technology—all within a compressed developmental window (Brat et al., 2017). The convergence of these stressors renders university students particularly vulnerable to psychological distress, a multidimensional construct encompassing symptoms of anxiety, depression, somatization, and general emotional suffering (Kessler et al., 2002).

Empirical evidence has consistently documented elevated rates of psychological distress among university populations across diverse cultural and geographic contexts. Tang et al. (2020) reported alarming prevalence rates of depressive symptoms (9.0%) and post-traumatic stress symptoms (2.7%) among Chinese university students during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. In the Indian context, Das et al. (2023) documented significant levels of psychological distress among both medical and non-medical students in West Bengal during the late phase of the pandemic, while Stanley and Mettilda Bhuvaneswari (2016) highlighted the considerable stress and anxiety experienced by social work students in India. More recently, Hossain et al. (2025) explored the interplay between psychological well-being and self-esteem among higher education students in West Bengal, underscoring the multifaceted nature of student mental health challenges in the Indian subcontinent.

The COVID-19 pandemic served as a catalyst that amplified pre-existing vulnerabilities and introduced novel stressors, including social isolation, academic disruption, economic uncertainty, and health anxiety (Rathakrishnan et al., 2022; Basak & Sinha, 2020; Kurniawan et al., 2024). While the acute phase of the pandemic has subsided, its psychosocial reverberations continue to shape the mental health landscape of university students, necessitating sustained research attention and evidence-based intervention strategies.

## 1.2 Self-Compassion: Conceptual Framework

Self-compassion, as conceptualized by Kristin Neff (2003), represents a healthy and adaptive form of self-relating that involves treating oneself with kindness and understanding during moments of suffering, failure, or perceived inadequacy. Neff's tripartite model delineates three core, interrelated components:

1. **Self-Kindness versus Self-Judgment:** The tendency to be warm, understanding, and supportive toward oneself rather than harshly self-critical when confronting personal shortcomings or painful experiences.

2. **Common Humanity versus Isolation:** The recognition that suffering and imperfection are shared aspects of the human experience, rather than perceiving one's struggles as isolating and uniquely personal.

3. **Mindfulness versus Over-Identification:** The capacity to hold one's painful thoughts and emotions in balanced, non-judgmental awareness, without suppressing or exaggerating them.

Self-compassion has been robustly linked to a wide array of positive psychological outcomes. Ferrari et al. (2019), in a comprehensive meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, demonstrated that self-compassion interventions yield significant improvements in psychosocial outcomes, including reductions in depression, anxiety, and psychological distress. Galino Buen et al. (2026) extended this evidence through a meta-analysis specifically examining mindfulness- and self-compassion-based interventions (MBSCIs) among university students, confirming their efficacy in reducing psychological distress. Wang et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review elucidating the mechanisms underlying the self-compassion–psychological outcomes relationship, identifying cognitive reappraisal, emotion regulation, and reduced rumination as key mediating pathways.

In the Indian context, research on self-compassion remains relatively nascent but growing. Jain et al. (2025) conducted a randomized controlled trial demonstrating the positive effects of an online self-compassion intervention on Indian students, while Iyer et al. (2022) explored the role of self-compassion in mediating the relationship between internet addiction, aggression, and psychological well-being among Indian populations. These studies provide promising preliminary evidence for the relevance and applicability of self-compassion constructs within the Indian cultural milieu.

The theoretical potency of self-compassion as a protective factor against psychological distress is further substantiated by research demonstrating its mediating role in various psychopathological pathways. Liu and Wang (2026) found that self-compassion mediates the relationship between academic burnout and depression among college students. Liu et al. (2024) demonstrated that self-compassion, in conjunction with hope, contributes to psychological flourishing among college students, with emotion regulation serving as a moderating variable. Dehshiri (2024) provided evidence that self-compassion influences anxiety and depression symptoms through the mediation of shame and guilt, highlighting the complex affective mechanisms through which self-compassion exerts its protective effects.

## 1.3 Psychological Distress: Conceptual Understanding

Psychological distress is a broad construct that refers to a state of emotional suffering characterized by symptoms of depression and anxiety that may interfere with daily functioning (Kessler et al., 2002). Unlike clinical diagnoses, psychological distress exists on a continuum and captures the sub-threshold and transdiagnostic emotional difficulties experienced by individuals in response to stressors.

Among university students, psychological distress manifests through diverse symptom presentations, including persistent worry, sleep disturbances, concentration difficulties, feelings of hopelessness, social withdrawal, and somatic complaints. The consequences of untreated psychological distress extend beyond the individual, affecting academic performance, interpersonal relationships, institutional retention rates, and societal productivity (Rathakrishnan et al., 2022).

Several factors have been implicated in the etiology and maintenance of psychological distress among university students:

- **Academic stressors:** Examination pressure, performance expectations, and competitive academic environments (Liu & Wang, 2026).
- **Social stressors:** Relationship difficulties, social comparison, peer pressure, and perceived discrimination (Li et al., 2022).
- **Developmental transitions:** Identity consolidation, autonomy development, and separation from family systems (Clarke, 2024; Kroshus et al., 2021).
- **Pandemic-related stressors:** Social isolation, online learning challenges, health anxiety, and economic uncertainty (Tang et al., 2020; Das et al., 2023).

Understanding the protective factors that attenuate psychological distress is therefore of critical importance for informing preventive and therapeutic interventions within higher education settings.

## 1.4 Perceived Social Support: Theoretical Perspectives

Perceived social support refers to an individual's subjective evaluation of the availability and adequacy of supportive resources from their social network, including family, friends, and significant others (Zimet et al., 1988). Importantly, perceived social support is distinguished from received (enacted) support; it is the *perception* of support availability—rather than the actual receipt of support—that has been most consistently associated with positive mental health outcomes (Shelton et al., 2017).

Theoretical frameworks underpinning the study of perceived social support include:

- **The Stress-Buffering Model** (Cohen & Wills, 1985): This model posits that social support protects individuals from the deleterious effects of stressful events by providing resources that facilitate adaptive coping. Support may buffer stress by altering cognitive appraisals of threatening situations (preventing stress appraisals) or by providing solutions to problems, reducing the perceived importance of the problem, or facilitating healthful behaviors.

- **The Main Effect Model:** This perspective holds that social support has a direct, beneficial effect on well-being regardless of stress levels, by providing positive experiences, stable social roles, and a sense of belonging and predictability.

Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates the protective role of perceived social support against psychological distress among university students. Rathakrishnan et al. (2022) found that perceived social support was associated with adaptive coping strategies and lower psychological distress among university students in Malaysia during the COVID-19 pandemic. Tannous-Haddad et al. (2024) demonstrated that the relationship between perceived social support and psychological well-being is mediated by self-compassion and moderated by cultural factors, highlighting the importance of examining these constructs within specific cultural contexts. Ee et al. (2026) found that perceived social support predicted subjective happiness among undergraduate counseling students in Malaysia, with self-esteem serving as a mediating variable.

In the Indian context, Basak and Sinha (2020) established associations between interpersonal social support and reduced depression among undergraduate college students in Kolkata, providing culturally relevant evidence for the protective role of social support. Shelton et al. (2017) further demonstrated that cultural orientations moderate the relationship between perceived social support and mental health outcomes, underscoring the necessity of culturally informed research.

## 1.5 The Moderating Role of Perceived Social Support

While the independent contributions of self-compassion and perceived social support to psychological well-being have been well-documented, the interaction between these two protective factors remains insufficiently explored. The present study is grounded in the theoretical proposition that perceived social support functions as a **moderator** of the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress.

The moderation hypothesis is informed by several converging lines of evidence:

1. **Complementary protective mechanisms:** Self-compassion represents an *intrapersonal* resource (how one relates to oneself), while perceived social support constitutes an *interpersonal* resource (how one perceives relational support). The interaction between intrapersonal and interpersonal protective factors may produce synergistic effects that exceed their individual contributions (Ross & Ross, 2025; Masoumi et al., 2022).

2. **Social facilitation of self-compassion:** Environments rich in social support may create conditions that facilitate the development and expression of self-compassion. When individuals perceive that they are supported and cared for by others, they may be more likely to internalize compassionate attitudes toward themselves (Barclay, 2024; Zhou et al., 2022).

3. **Differential efficacy across support levels:** The protective effect of self-compassion may be differentially potent depending on the level of perceived social support. For students with high perceived social support, self-compassion may operate within a reinforcing relational context that amplifies its protective effects. Conversely, for students with low perceived social support, self-compassion may need to compensate for the absence of external relational resources, potentially attenuating its protective efficacy (Hayes et al., 2023; Habibi et al., 2023).

4. **Empirical precedents:** Several studies have examined related moderation models. Yang et al. (2023) found that self-compassion mediates the relationship between perceived social support and procrastination, with negative emotions serving as a sequential mediator. Liu and Wang (2026) demonstrated that perceived social support moderates the relationship between academic burnout and depression, with self-compassion functioning as a mediator. These findings suggest complex interactional dynamics between self-compassion and perceived social support that warrant further investigation through moderation analysis.

## 1.6 Rationale for the Present Study

Despite the growing body of research on self-compassion, psychological distress, and perceived social support individually and in dyadic relationships, significant gaps persist in the literature:

1. **Limited moderation studies:** Few studies have explicitly examined perceived social support as a moderator of the self-compassion–psychological distress relationship. The majority of existing research has examined these variables in mediation frameworks (e.g., Yang et al., 2023; Tannous-Haddad et al., 2024), leaving the moderation hypothesis largely untested.

2. **Paucity of Indian research:** The preponderance of existing research has been conducted in Western (e.g., United States, Europe) and East Asian (e.g., China, Malaysia) contexts. Research on self-compassion and its interactions with social support among Indian university students remains critically sparse (Jain et al., 2025; Iyer et al., 2022; Hossain et al., 2025).

3. **Post-pandemic context:** The post-pandemic period presents a unique psychosocial landscape in which the mental health needs of university students may differ qualitatively from pre-pandemic norms. Research conducted in this context can inform temporally relevant intervention strategies.

4. **Intervention design implications:** Understanding whether perceived social support moderates the protective effects of self-compassion has direct implications for intervention design. If the moderation effect is confirmed, interventions may need to be tailored to the social support context of the individual, rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach.

## 1.9 Purpose of the Study

The overarching purpose of the present study is to empirically examine the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress among university students in India, with a specific focus on investigating the moderating role of perceived social support in this relationship. By elucidating the interactional dynamics between an intrapersonal protective factor (self-compassion) and an interpersonal protective factor (perceived social support), this study aims to generate evidence that can inform targeted, context-sensitive psychosocial interventions for the university student population.
