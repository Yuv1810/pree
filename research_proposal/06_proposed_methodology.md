# Proposed Methodology

---

## 3.1 Aim of the Study

The present study aims to investigate the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress among university students in India, and to examine the moderating role of perceived social support in this relationship.

---

## 3.2 Objectives of the Study

The specific objectives of the study are:

1. To assess the levels of self-compassion, psychological distress, and perceived social support among university students.
2. To examine the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress among university students.
3. To examine the relationship between perceived social support and psychological distress among university students.
4. To examine the relationship between self-compassion and perceived social support among university students.
5. To investigate the moderating role of perceived social support in the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress among university students.
6. To examine the differences in self-compassion, psychological distress, and perceived social support across demographic variables (gender, age, year of study, discipline of study, residential status).
7. To explore whether the dimensions of perceived social support (family, friends, significant others) differentially moderate the self-compassion–psychological distress relationship.

---

## 3.3 Need for the Study (Research Gap)

The present study addresses several critical research gaps identified in the literature:

### Gap 1: Moderation Models Remain Untested

The majority of existing studies have examined self-compassion, perceived social support, and psychological distress using **mediation** frameworks (e.g., Tannous-Haddad et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022; Masoumi et al., 2022). While mediation analysis reveals the pathways *through which* variables exert their effects, **moderation analysis** reveals the *boundary conditions* under which relationships are strengthened or attenuated. The specific question of whether perceived social support moderates the self-compassion–psychological distress relationship has not been adequately addressed.

### Gap 2: Paucity of Indian Research

A disproportionate share of existing research has been conducted with samples from Western (United States, Europe, Australia) and East Asian (China, Malaysia, South Korea) populations. The Indian university student population—one of the largest in the world with approximately 43 million students enrolled in higher education (UGC, 2023)—remains critically **underrepresented** in the self-compassion literature. Only a handful of studies (Jain et al., 2025; Iyer et al., 2022; Hossain et al., 2025; Stanley & Mettilda Bhuvaneswari, 2016; Basak & Sinha, 2020) have examined these constructs among Indian students, and none have tested the proposed moderation model.

### Gap 3: Post-Pandemic Psychosocial Context

The post-pandemic period (2024–2026) presents a unique psychosocial landscape characterized by altered social interaction patterns, persistent academic and economic uncertainties, and shifting mental health needs (Das et al., 2023). Research conducted in this context can provide temporally relevant data to inform current intervention efforts.

### Gap 4: Multidimensional Social Support as Moderator

Most studies treat perceived social support as a **unidimensional** construct. The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; Zimet et al., 1988) assesses three distinct dimensions—family support, friend support, and significant other support—each of which may have differential moderating effects on the self-compassion–distress relationship. This multidimensional moderation analysis has not been systematically conducted.

### Gap 5: Intervention Design Implications

Without understanding whether and how perceived social support moderates the protective effects of self-compassion, the design of psychosocial interventions remains suboptimal. If moderation is confirmed, interventions can be **personalized** based on the individual's social support context, rather than employing a uniform approach (Stutts, 2022; Brat et al., 2017).

## 3.4 Hypotheses

Based on the review of literature and the theoretical framework, the following hypotheses are proposed:

### Primary Hypotheses

| # | Hypothesis | Direction |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| **H₁** | Self-compassion is significantly negatively correlated with psychological distress among university students. | Negative |
| **H₂** | Perceived social support is significantly negatively correlated with psychological distress among university students. | Negative |
| **H₃** | Self-compassion is significantly positively correlated with perceived social support among university students. | Positive |
| **H₄** | Perceived social support significantly moderates the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress among university students, such that the negative relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress is stronger among students with higher levels of perceived social support. | Interaction |

### Secondary Hypotheses

| # | Hypothesis | Direction |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| **H₅** | There are significant differences in self-compassion, psychological distress, and perceived social support across gender (male, female, non-binary). | Group difference |
| **H₆** | There are significant differences in self-compassion, psychological distress, and perceived social support across year of study (1st year through final year / postgraduate). | Group difference |
| **H₇** | The three dimensions of perceived social support (family, friends, significant others) differentially moderate the self-compassion–psychological distress relationship. | Differential moderation |

### Null Hypotheses

| # | Null Hypothesis |
|---|----------------|
| **H₀₁** | There is no significant relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress among university students. |
| **H₀₂** | There is no significant relationship between perceived social support and psychological distress among university students. |
| **H₀₃** | There is no significant relationship between self-compassion and perceived social support among university students. |
| **H₀₄** | Perceived social support does not significantly moderate the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress among university students. |

## 3.5 Study Design

### 3.5.1 Research Design

The present study employs a **cross-sectional, quantitative, correlational research design** with a moderation analysis framework.

- **Cross-sectional:** Data will be collected at a single time point, providing a snapshot of the relationships among variables.
- **Quantitative:** Data will be collected through standardized psychometric instruments yielding numerical scores amenable to statistical analysis.
- **Correlational with moderation:** The study examines associations among variables and tests whether the strength/direction of one relationship (self-compassion → psychological distress) varies as a function of a third variable (perceived social support).

### 3.5.2 Design Justification

A cross-sectional design is appropriate for the following reasons:
1. The primary aim is to test the moderation hypothesis, which requires examining the statistical interaction between self-compassion and perceived social support in predicting psychological distress.
2. The design is feasible within the time and resource constraints of the doctoral research program.
3. Cross-sectional designs are consistent with the methodological approaches used in the majority of referenced studies (e.g., Ross & Ross, 2025; Hayes et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023).

## 3.6 Sample

### 3.6.1 Target Population

The target population consists of **university students** currently enrolled in undergraduate (UG) and postgraduate (PG) degree programs across universities in India.

### 3.6.2 Accessible Population

The accessible population consists of university students enrolled in universities located in **West Bengal and neighboring eastern Indian states** (e.g., Jharkhand, Odisha, Bihar), aged 18–30 years.

### 3.6.3 Sample Size

The sample size has been determined using **a priori power analysis** (G*Power 3.1; Faul et al., 2009) with the following parameters:

| Parameter | Value | Justification |
|-----------|-------|---------------|
| **Test family** | F-test (Linear multiple regression: R² increase) | Moderation analysis uses ΔR² |
| **Effect size f²** | 0.05 (small-to-medium) | Conservative estimate based on Ferrari et al. (2019) and Ross & Ross (2025) |
| **α (Type I error)** | 0.05 | Standard significance level |
| **Power (1 − β)** | 0.90 | High power to minimize Type II error |
| **Number of predictors** | 5 (SC, PSS, SC×PSS, and 2 covariates) | Main effects, interaction, and demographic covariates |
| **Minimum sample (computed)** | 262 | G*Power output |
| **Attrition buffer** | 35% | To account for incomplete responses and data quality issues |
| **Final target sample** | **N = 400** | 262 + 35% buffer ≈ 354; rounded up to 400 for statistical robustness |

### 3.6.4 Sampling Technique

A **multi-stage stratified random sampling** procedure will be employed:

```
Stage 1: Purposive Selection of Universities
├── University Type: Public (2) + Private (2) = 4 universities
└── Justification: Representation of diverse institutional contexts

Stage 2: Stratified Selection of Faculties/Departments
├── Strata: Arts/Humanities, Sciences, Commerce/Business, Professional Programs
└── From each university: 2–3 departments selected randomly

Stage 3: Random Selection of Students
├── Within each selected department, students are randomly selected
│   from class enrollment lists
└── Proportional allocation across strata (gender, year of study)

Final: N = 400 students (approximately 100 per university)
```

## 3.7 Operational Definitions

### 3.7.1 Self-Compassion (Independent Variable — X)

**Operational Definition:** Self-compassion is operationally defined as the total score obtained on the **Self-Compassion Scale (SCS; Neff, 2003)**. The SCS measures six components: Self-Kindness, Self-Judgment (reverse-scored), Common Humanity, Isolation (reverse-scored), Mindfulness, and Over-Identification (reverse-scored). Higher total scores indicate higher levels of self-compassion. For the purpose of this study, self-compassion is treated as a continuous variable.

### 3.7.2 Psychological Distress (Dependent Variable — Y)

**Operational Definition:** Psychological distress is operationally defined as the total score obtained on the **Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10; Kessler et al., 2002)**. The K10 measures non-specific psychological distress over the past 30 days, assessing symptoms of anxiety and depression. Scores range from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating higher levels of psychological distress. Categorical cut-offs are also available:

| Score Range | Classification |
|-------------|---------------|
| 10–15 | Low (likely to be well) |
| 16–21 | Moderate distress |
| 22–29 | High distress |
| 30–50 | Very high distress (likely to have a severe mental disorder) |

### 3.7.3 Perceived Social Support (Moderator Variable — W)

**Operational Definition:** Perceived social support is operationally defined as the total score and sub-dimension scores obtained on the **Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; Zimet et al., 1988)**. The MSPSS assesses perceived support from three sources: Family, Friends, and Significant Others. Higher scores indicate higher levels of perceived social support. For the moderation analysis, total MSPSS scores will be used as the primary moderator variable, with sub-dimension scores examined in supplementary analyses.

### 3.7.4 University Students

**Operational Definition:** University students are defined as individuals aged 18–30 years who are currently enrolled as full-time students in undergraduate (bachelor's degree) or postgraduate (master's degree) programs at recognized universities in India, attending regular on-campus classes.

### 3.8.2 Self-Compassion Scale (SCS; Neff, 2003)

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Author** | Kristin D. Neff (2003) |
| **Items** | 26 items |
| **Subscales** | 6 subscales: Self-Kindness (5 items), Self-Judgment (5 items), Common Humanity (4 items), Isolation (4 items), Mindfulness (4 items), Over-Identification (4 items) |
| **Response Format** | 5-point Likert scale (1 = Almost Never to 5 = Almost Always) |
| **Scoring** | Mean of all 26 items after reverse-scoring negative subscales (Self-Judgment, Isolation, Over-Identification). Total score range: 1–5. |
| **Interpretation** | Higher scores = Higher self-compassion |
| **Reliability** | Internal consistency: α = .92 (total); Test-retest reliability (3 weeks): *r* = .93 (Neff, 2003) |
| **Validity** | Strong convergent validity with emotional intelligence, social connectedness; discriminant validity from self-esteem (Neff, 2003) |
| **Indian Validation** | Validated in Indian samples (Jain et al., 2025; Iyer et al., 2022) |
| **Time to Complete** | 5–8 minutes |

**Sample Items:**
- "I try to be understanding and patient toward aspects of my personality I don't like." (Self-Kindness)
- "When I'm feeling down, I tend to feel like most other people are probably happier than I am." (Isolation; reverse-scored)

### 3.8.3 Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10; Kessler et al., 2002)

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Author** | Ronald C. Kessler et al. (2002) |
| **Items** | 10 items |
| **Subscales** | Unidimensional (non-specific psychological distress) |
| **Response Format** | 5-point Likert scale (1 = None of the time to 5 = All of the time) |
| **Scoring** | Sum of all 10 items. Total score range: 10–50. |
| **Interpretation** | Higher scores = Higher psychological distress |
| **Reliability** | Internal consistency: α = .89–.93 across populations; Strong test-retest reliability |
| **Validity** | Excellent criterion validity for detecting DSM-IV anxiety and depressive disorders; validated cross-culturally |
| **Indian Validation** | Used extensively in Indian research (Das et al., 2023; Hossain et al., 2025) |
| **Time to Complete** | 2–3 minutes |

**Sample Items:**
- "During the last 30 days, about how often did you feel nervous?"
- "During the last 30 days, about how often did you feel hopeless?"

### 3.8.4 Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; Zimet et al., 1988)

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Author** | Gregory D. Zimet, Nancy W. Dahlem, Sara G. Zimet, & Gordon K. Farley (1988) |
| **Items** | 12 items |
| **Subscales** | 3 subscales: Family (4 items), Friends (4 items), Significant Others (4 items) |
| **Response Format** | 7-point Likert scale (1 = Very Strongly Disagree to 7 = Very Strongly Agree) |
| **Scoring** | Mean of all 12 items for total score; mean of respective 4 items for subscale scores. Total score range: 1–7. |
| **Interpretation** | Higher scores = Higher perceived social support. Subscale scores range: 1–7. |
| **Reliability** | Internal consistency: α = .88 (total), .87 (Family), .85 (Friends), .91 (Significant Others); Good test-retest reliability |
| **Validity** | Strong factorial validity (3-factor structure confirmed cross-culturally); convergent validity with other social support measures |
| **Indian Validation** | Used in Indian research (Basak & Sinha, 2020) |
| **Time to Complete** | 2–3 minutes |

**Sample Items:**
- "My family really tries to help me." (Family)
- "I can count on my friends when things go wrong." (Friends)
- "There is a special person in my life who cares about my feelings." (Significant Others)

## 3.9 Procedure

### 3.9.1 Ethical Approval

Prior to data collection, ethical approval will be obtained from the **Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC)** of the researcher's university. The study will adhere to the ethical guidelines prescribed by the **American Psychological Association (APA, 2017)** and the **Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR, 2017)** guidelines for biomedical and health research involving human participants.

Key ethical considerations include:

- **Informed consent:** Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The consent form will clearly describe the study's purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality protocols, and the voluntary nature of participation.
- **Confidentiality:** All data will be anonymized using participant codes. No personally identifiable information will be linked to the questionnaire responses.
- **Right to withdraw:** Participants will be informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.
- **Debriefing:** Upon completion of data collection, participants will receive a debriefing sheet explaining the study's objectives and providing contact information for campus counseling services, should they experience any distress.
- **Data security:** All data will be stored on password-protected devices and encrypted cloud storage, accessible only to the research team.

### 3.9.2 Pilot Study

A **pilot study** will be conducted with a sample of 30–40 university students (not included in the final sample) to:

1. Assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire battery.
2. Estimate the time required for questionnaire completion.
3. Identify any logistical or procedural issues.
4. Compute preliminary reliability coefficients for the instruments in the study context.
5. Verify the data entry and analysis procedures.

### 3.9.4 Data Collection Strategy

Data will be collected through both **offline (pen-and-paper)** and **online (digital)** modalities to maximize response rates and accommodate diverse student preferences:

**Offline (Primary):**
- Standardized questionnaire packets will be administered in classroom settings during scheduled class periods.
- The researcher and trained research assistants will be present to address any queries.
- Completed questionnaires will be collected immediately upon completion.

**Online (Supplementary):**
- An identical digital version of the questionnaire battery will be created using **Qualtrics** or **Google Forms**.
- The online link will be distributed to students who are unable to attend in-person sessions.
- IP address tracking will be enabled to prevent duplicate submissions.
- A consent checkbox will replace the physical consent form.

### 3.10.4 Moderation Analysis: Detailed Specification

The moderation analysis will be conducted using **Hayes' PROCESS Macro v4.2 for SPSS** (Hayes, 2022), Model 1.

**Model Specification:**

```
Y (Psychological Distress) = b₀ + b₁X (Self-Compassion) + b₂W (Perceived Social Support) 
                              + b₃(X × W) + b₄(Covariate₁) + b₅(Covariate₂) + ε

Where:
- X  = Self-Compassion (mean-centered)
- W  = Perceived Social Support (mean-centered)
- X×W = Interaction term (product of mean-centered X and W)
- Y  = Psychological Distress (K10 total score)
- b₃  = Moderation effect (key test)
- Covariates: Gender (dummy-coded), Age (continuous)
```

**Moderation Probing:**
- If *b₃* is statistically significant (*p* < .05), the moderation effect will be probed using the **Johnson-Neyman technique** and **simple slopes analysis** at three levels of the moderator: −1 *SD* (low PSS), Mean (moderate PSS), and +1 *SD* (high PSS).
- Confidence intervals (95% CI) and bootstrapped standard errors (5,000 bootstrap samples) will be reported.

**Visualization:**
- Interaction plots will be generated depicting the relationship between self-compassion and psychological distress at low, moderate, and high levels of perceived social support.
